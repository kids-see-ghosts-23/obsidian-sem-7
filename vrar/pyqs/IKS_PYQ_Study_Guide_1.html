Unit 1: Introduction to Indian Knowledge Systems

1.1 Perception and Prologue ⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

  • IKS (Indian Knowledge Systems) is all the knowledge and practices India developed over thousands of years. It covers philosophy, maths, medicine (Ayurveda), arts, architecture, farming and understanding nature.
  • It was passed from generation to generation and helped people live in harmony with nature and society.
  • Under colonial education, Indian knowledge was pushed aside and Western ideas took over schools and colleges. Students lost touch with their roots.
  • NEP 2020 wants to bring IKS back at all levels of education: interdisciplinary learning, Indian languages, and traditional wisdom for sustainable development.
  • Its success depends on how students perceive IKS, which is why this topic is called "perception".
  • Old + new together is the idea: traditional farming with modern tools, or Ayurveda with modern healthcare.
Likely · 2–3M⭐⭐
What is IKS? Why do we need to study it today?

IKS is the knowledge India built up over thousands of years in philosophy, science, maths, medicine, arts, architecture and agriculture. It was passed down through generations.

Why study it today:

  • It reconnects us with our roots, which colonial education cut off.
  • It gives sustainable solutions, such as organic farming and water conservation.
  • It supports health and wellness through Yoga and Ayurveda.
  • It can be combined with modern science for innovation. Pāṇini's grammar, for example, is useful for computers.
  • NEP 2020 makes it part of education.

  • It recognises the gap created by colonial education.
  • It brings IKS into the curriculum at all levels.
  • It encourages interdisciplinary learning and Indian languages.
  • It treats traditional wisdom as important for sustainable development and cultural preservation.

1.2 Historicity of IKS ⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

"Historicity" means how old IKS is and how it developed over time. Think of it as a timeline:

StageWhat happened
Vedic period (~1500–500 BCE)4 Vedas composed. Upaniṣads discuss Ātman (self) and Brahman (ultimate reality).
EpicsRāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata teach morals. The Bhagavad Gītā (part of the Mahābhārata) is a philosophy text.
Classical worksKālidāsa's Śākuntalam (literature), Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras (well-being).
Philosophy6 schools: Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta.
MathsĀryabhaṭa, Brahmagupta, Bhāskarācārya: zero, decimal system, trigonometry. Brahmagupta gave rules for quadratic equations.
AstronomyĀryabhaṭa: Earth rotates, eclipses explained, Earth's circumference. Varāhamihira's Bṛhat Saṃhitā mixes astronomy and weather.
MedicineCharaka Saṃhitā (internal medicine), Suśruta Saṃhitā (surgery).
UniversitiesTakṣaśilā (~600 BCE: medicine, law, military science). Nālandā (~5th c. CE, library Dharmaganja, students from China, Korea, Central Asia).
ManuscriptsPalm leaves and birch bark kept in temples and monasteries. Scripts: Brāhmī, Kharoṣṭhī, Devanāgarī.
Architecture textsMānasāra, Mayamata, Samarāṅgaṇa-sūtradhāra.
Likely · 3–5M⭐⭐
Write a note on the historicity of Indian Knowledge Systems.

IKS is one of the oldest knowledge traditions in the world. Its journey looks like this:

  • Vedic roots: it starts with the 4 Vedas (~1500–500 BCE). The Upaniṣads asked deep questions about the self (Ātman) and reality (Brahman).
  • Epics and classics: the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata taught values. The Gītā gave philosophy, and Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras gave a path to mental well-being.
  • Philosophy: six schools developed logic, atomic theory, mind–matter ideas and spirituality.
  • Science and maths: Āryabhaṭa (Earth's rotation, eclipses), Brahmagupta (quadratic equations, zero), Bhāskarācārya. Medicine came through Charaka and Suśruta.
  • Education centres: Takṣaśilā and Nālandā attracted foreign students. Nālandā had a huge library, Dharmaganja.
  • Preservation: knowledge was saved on palm-leaf and birch-bark manuscripts in Brāhmī, Kharoṣṭhī and Devanāgarī.

This shows that IKS was a living, organised system of knowledge across many fields, not just religion.

Likely · 2M⭐
Write a short note on Nālandā and Takṣaśilā.
  • Takṣaśilā (~600 BCE) is one of the earliest universities. It taught medicine, law and military science.
  • Nālandā (~5th century CE) was a global hub of education. Its huge library was called Dharmaganja, and scholars came from China, Korea and Central Asia.

1.3 Indian Traditional Knowledge ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

  • Traditional knowledge (TK), also called indigenous knowledge, is the knowledge, know-how, skills and practices that local communities developed and passed from one generation to the next.
  • It is often part of the community's cultural or spiritual identity.
  • Fields: agriculture, science, technology, ecology, medicine, biodiversity.
  • It includes the knowledge itself and cultural expressions like signs and symbols.
  • Examples: Ayurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha and Naturopathy are recognised in India. So are indigenous farming methods and the use of herbs and spices.
  • How it is expressed: mostly orally, through stories, songs, folklore, proverbs, beliefs, rituals, dances, paintings and carvings. It belongs to the group, not to one person.
  • International bodies like the UN and WIPO have recognised TK.
Likely · 2–3M⭐⭐⭐
What is traditional knowledge? How is it passed on / expressed?

Meaning: Traditional knowledge is the knowledge, skills and practices that a community develops from long experience and hands down from generation to generation. Examples are Ayurveda, Yoga, indigenous farming methods and herbal remedies.

How it is passed on / expressed:

  • Orally: elders teach the young by word of mouth.
  • Through stories, songs, folklore and proverbs.
  • Through rituals, beliefs and cultural values.
  • Through arts: dance, painting, carving.
  • Through practice: learning by doing, for example in farming or crafts.

It belongs to the whole community, and it keeps adapting to the local culture and environment.

1.6 Threats to TK and the Need to Protect It ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

TK has been exploited for centuries, first by colonisation, then by capitalism (MNCs) and consumerism.

  • Language threat: TK is stored in local languages and Sanskrit. When the language dies, the knowledge dies too.
  • External pressures: migration, environmental damage, modern lifestyles and globalisation break the chain of passing knowledge down.
  • Biopiracy: commercial use and patenting of natural products or genetic material without permission or benefit-sharing. Example: the turmeric patent.

Why protect it: culture and identity, livelihood, health benefits and ecological benefits.

PYQ · CIE-1 · 2M⭐⭐⭐
Explain any two threats to traditional knowledge.
  1. Biopiracy: companies patent and sell natural products or knowledge that local people have used for centuries, without permission and without sharing profits. Example: a US patent was granted on the wound-healing use of turmeric, even though Ayurveda has used it for thousands of years. It was cancelled after India challenged it.
  2. Language loss: much TK is stored in local languages and Sanskrit, for example Ayurveda texts. When people stop using these languages, the knowledge inside them is lost too.

Third one (backup): External pressures such as migration, globalisation and modern lifestyles mean young people no longer learn the traditional ways.

1.7 Transition from Traditional to Western Knowledge ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

Gurukula / Nālandā / Takṣaśilā → British rule: English education → Divide between IKS and Western science → Today: NEP 2020 brings them together
  • Earlier: Gurukula and temple-based learning. Education was holistic, mixing philosophy, science, arts and ethics. Knowledge was sacred (vidyā) and aimed at self-realisation and social harmony, not money. It was passed on orally and through Sanskrit texts.
  • British period: English language, new curricula and modern institutions. The focus moved to empirical, utilitarian, job-oriented learning.
  • Result: a gap opened between IKS and Western science.
  • Good side: Indian heritage got documented and became known globally.
  • Now: both are being combined again.

1.8 The IKS Corpus: A Classification Framework ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

"Corpus" means the whole collection of IKS. It is divided into Literary (written) and Non-Literary (oral / practised).

  • Sanātana Dharma corpus:
    • Core = Vedas and Vedāṅgas (the 14 Vidyāsthānas) plus works of religious leaders and philosophers.
    • Other = maths, astronomy, engineering, architecture, alchemy, aesthetics, Kāvyas, health, psychology, administration, code of living. Mostly in Sanskrit, later retold in regional languages.
    • Example: maths used in astronomy for Yuga cycles shows that science and metaphysics were linked.
  • Other Dhārmic literature (Buddhist and Jain, ~500 BCE onward):
    • Buddhist: maths, shipbuilding, maritime activity, alchemy. Rasaratnākara by Nāgārjuna (1st c. CE) is an early alchemy text.
    • Jain: maths as a core subject (Gaṇitānuyoga). Tattvārtha Sūtra. They even estimated the number of species as 296.
  • Regional: literature in all major Indian languages.
  • Non-literary: oral traditions such as the 64 Kalās (music, dance, painting, sculpture, crafts), health traditions, food and life practices, folklore.
  • Sciences in the corpus: Vṛkṣa Āyurveda (plant science), metalworking, shipbuilding, dams, watershed management, cosmetics, perfumes, dyes, town planning.
  • Health: Charaka Saṃhitā, Suśruta Saṃhitā, Aṣṭāṅga Hṛdaya.
  • Nītiśāstras (ethics and governance): Rāja-nīti (public administration) and Sāmānya-nīti (personal ethics and morality).

1.9 Vedic Corpus ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

Veda = knowledge, from the root vid. The root has 4 meanings, and all of them fit the Vedas: to know (jñāne), to think / discuss (vicāraṇe), to exist (sattāyām), and to gain (lābhe).

There are 4 Vedas, together called Chaturveda. They were passed on orally with great accuracy, and the Ṛg Veda is dated around 1600 BCE.

Each Veda has 4 parts:

Saṃhitā hymns/mantras → Brāhmaṇa explanation of rituals → Āraṇyaka philosophy behind rituals → Upaniṣad teacher–student talks, pure philosophy
VedaKey facts to remember
Ṛg Veda
(oldest, "praise")		10 Maṇḍalas, 1028 hymns (sūktas), ~10,552 verses. Maṇḍala → Anuvāka → Sūkta. Famous sūktas: Puruṣa (10.90), Nāsadīya (10.129, creation), Hiraṇyagarbha (10.121), Śrī, Bhū, Devī. 21 śākhās, 5 survive, 2 popular: Śākala, Bāṣkala. Aitareya Upaniṣad has the mahāvākya "Prajñānam Brahma".
Yajur Veda
("book of rituals")		Prose mantras chanted during yajña. Śukla ("bright": mantra and Brāhmaṇa kept separate, easier) and Kṛṣṇa ("dark": both mixed, harder). 101 śākhās, 6 survive: Kṛṣṇa (Taittirīya, Maitrāyaṇī, Kaṭha, Kapiṣṭhala), Śukla (Kāṇva, Mādhyandina).
Sāma Veda
("book of songs")		Ṛg verses set to music. Shortest: 1549 verses. Root of Indian classical music and dance. Upaniṣads: Chāndogya, Kena. 1000 recensions, 3 survive: Kauthuma, Jaiminīya, Rāṇāyanīya. Parts: Ārcika and Gāna.
Atharva Veda
("Veda of daily life / magic formulas")		730 hymns, ~6000 mantras, 20 books. Medicine, cures for disease, herbs. Example: treating an open fracture and wrapping it with the Rohiṇī plant. Upaniṣads: Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Praśna.
PYQ · CIE-1 · 3M⭐⭐⭐
How does the Vedic Corpus form the foundation of Indian knowledge traditions?

Veda means "knowledge" (from the root vid, to know). There are 4 Vedas: Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma and Atharva. Each has 4 layers: Saṃhitā, Brāhmaṇa, Āraṇyaka and Upaniṣad.

Why they are the foundation:

  • Language sciences: to protect the Vedas exactly, the Vedāṅgas were created. This gave us phonetics, grammar and etymology.
  • Maths and astronomy: the Śulba Sūtras (geometry for fire altars) and Jyotiṣa grew from Vedic rituals.
  • Philosophy: all six āstika schools accept the Vedas. Vedānta comes straight from the Upaniṣads.
  • Arts and medicine: the Sāma Veda is the root of Indian music. The Atharva Veda has early medicine.
  • Values: "Satyam vada, dharmam chara" (speak truth, follow dharma) comes from the Upaniṣads.

So almost every Indian knowledge stream starts from or is linked to the Vedas.

Likely · 4–5M⭐⭐⭐
Briefly describe the four Vedas.
  • Ṛg Veda: the oldest. A collection of 1028 hymns praising deities, in 10 Maṇḍalas. It discusses creation and cosmology (Nāsadīya Sūkta) and is still used in ceremonies.
  • Yajur Veda: the "book of rituals". Prose mantras a priest chants during yajña. It has two branches: Śukla (clear, separated) and Kṛṣṇa (mixed).
  • Sāma Veda: the "book of songs". Ṛg verses set to tunes. The shortest Veda (1549 verses) and the root of Indian music.
  • Atharva Veda: the "Veda of everyday life". 730 hymns in 20 books, with spells, medicine and herbal cures. It is closer to the daily life of common people.
Likely · 2–3M⭐⭐
What are the four parts (text types) of each Veda?
  1. Saṃhitā: the oldest layer. Mantras, hymns and prayers.
  2. Brāhmaṇa: commentary explaining the hymns and rituals.
  3. Āraṇyaka: "forest texts". The philosophy behind the rituals.
  4. Upaniṣad: conversations between teacher and student about the self and Brahman. This is the philosophical message of the Vedas.
🧠 "S-B-A-U" → Song, Book of rituals, Āraṇya (forest), Understanding
Likely · 2M⭐
What is the meaning of the word "Veda"? / Differentiate Śukla and Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda.

Veda comes from the root vid, meaning to know. It means knowledge. The root's four meanings (to know, to think, to exist, to gain) all describe the Vedas.

Śukla vs Kṛṣṇa: in Śukla ("bright") Yajur Veda the mantras and the Brāhmaṇa (explanation) are kept separate, so it is easier to understand. In Kṛṣṇa ("dark") they are mixed together, so it is harder.

1.10 Vedāṅgas (Limbs of the Vedas) ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

Vedāṅga = "limb of the Veda". Just as limbs support the body, these 6 subjects help us pronounce, understand, interpret and use the Vedas correctly. They are the last treatises of Vedic literature.

VedāṅgaSubjectBody partKey points
ŚikṣāPhoneticsNose 👃 (life-breath of mantras)Correct pronunciation and accent. A wrong sound changes the meaning and result. Śikṣāvallī lists 6: Varṇa, Svara, Mātrā, Balam, Sāma, Santāna.
ChandasMeterFeet / legs 🦶Counts pādas and syllables. Gāyatrī = 3 × 8 = 24 syllables. Anuṣṭubh = 4 × 8 = 32 (the typical śloka).
VyākaraṇaGrammarMouth / face 👄Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī. 14 Maheśvara sūtras (from Śiva's ḍamaru). Commentary: Patañjali's Mahābhāṣya (also Vararuchi).
NiruktaEtymologyEars 👂Meaning and origin of Vedic words. Yāska (~600 BCE) wrote on the Nighaṇṭu (glossary of hard words). Idea: all names come from verbs.
JyotiṣaAstronomyEyes 👁️Timing of rituals, cosmic rhythm. Varāhamihira's Pañca Siddhāntikā (5 siddhāntas: Pitāmaha, Vasiṣṭha, Romaka, Pauliśa, Sūrya). Bhāskarācārya's Pañcāṅga: Tithi, Vāra, Nakṣatra, Yoga, Karaṇa.
KalpaRitual manualHands ✋How to do rituals: which mantra, how many priests, what vessel. Sages: Āpastamba, Baudhāyana. 4 sūtras: Śrauta (big sacrifices), Gṛhya (home ceremonies), Dharma (social laws), Śulba (altar measurement / geometry).

Explain the six Vedāṅgas in brief.
  • Śikṣā (phonetics): teaches how to pronounce Vedic sounds correctly, with the right accent and length. A small mistake in sound can change the meaning. It is called the nose of the Veda.
  • Chandas (meter): tells how many lines (pādas) and syllables a verse has. Example: Gāyatrī has 3 lines × 8 syllables = 24.
  • Vyākaraṇa (grammar): rules for building words and sentences. Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī is the main work. It is called the mouth of the Veda.
  • Nirukta (etymology): explains where a word comes from and what it really means. Yāska worked on the Nighaṇṭu. It says all nouns come from verbs.
  • Jyotiṣa (astronomy): finds the right time for rituals by studying the sun, moon and stars. Varāhamihira's Pañca Siddhāntikā. The Pañcāṅga calendar.
  • Kalpa (rituals): a step-by-step manual for rituals. It has 4 sūtras: Śrauta, Gṛhya, Dharma and Śulba (geometry of altars).

Mention one key feature that differentiates Nyāya from Sāṅkhya philosophy.

Nyāya is mainly about logic and valid knowledge. It explains how to know the truth using 4 pramāṇas (perception, inference, comparison, testimony).

Sāṅkhya is about dualism. Everything comes from two realities, Puruṣa (consciousness) and Prakṛti (matter). Liberation comes when you realise you are Puruṣa, not Prakṛti. It is non-theistic.

In one line: Nyāya = how we know (logic). Sāṅkhya = what exists (Puruṣa + Prakṛti).

Explain the six āstika schools of Indian philosophy in brief.
  • Sāṅkhya (Kapila): the oldest. Reality = Puruṣa (self) + Prakṛti (matter). It is dualist. Prakṛti has 3 qualities (steadiness, activity, dullness). Liberation comes when you see that the self and matter are different.

Nyāya vs Vaiśeṣika: Nyāya focuses on logic and accepts 4 pramāṇas. Vaiśeṣika focuses on atoms / physical reality and accepts only 2 pramāṇas (perception, inference). Later they merged.

Jainism: based on 24 Tīrthaṅkaras. Ṛṣabhadeva was the first, and Pārśvanātha and Mahāvīra (6th c. BCE) are historical figures. 2.1 Linguistics: Pāṇini's Work on Sanskrit Grammar ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

  • Linguistics = the scientific study of language: speech sounds, grammar structures, meaning and syntax.
  • Pāṇini was born in Gandhāra and lived around the 5th century BCE. He is the father of linguistics.
  • His main work is the Aṣṭādhyāyī, with about 3983 sūtras covering all the patterns of Sanskrit.
  • Students used to memorise the whole thing, so it was an oral tradition.
  • He created a metalanguage (a language for describing language) that captures both sounds and grammar exactly.
  • His style is very short and compact, like the modern Backus–Naur Form (BNF) used in computer science. His analysis of word structure was ahead of any Western theory before the 20th century.
  • Why he wrote it: to preserve the correct interpretation of the Vedas and other texts.
  • Tri-Muni (three sages): Pāṇini (sūtras), Kātyāyana (Vārttikas, 4th c. BCE), Patañjali (Mahābhāṣya, 2nd c. BCE).
  • Pāṇini's grammar has 4 parts:
    1. Śiva Sūtras / Maheśvara Sūtras: 14 lines listing all sounds (phonology). Said to come from the sound of Naṭarāja's ḍamaru.
    2. Aṣṭādhyāyī: the main rules for word and sentence formation (morphology and syntax).
    3. Dhātupāṭha: a list of verb roots, grouped into classes.
    4. Gaṇapāṭha: lists of noun-stem classes.
Likely · 3–5M⭐⭐⭐
Explain Pāṇini's contribution to linguistics / Sanskrit grammar.
  • Pāṇini (Gandhāra, ~5th c. BCE) is called the father of linguistics.
  • He wrote the Aṣṭādhyāyī, a complete set of about 3983 rules that describes the whole Sanskrit language.
  • He built a whole system in 4 parts: Śiva Sūtras (sounds), Aṣṭādhyāyī (rules), Dhātupāṭha (verb roots) and Gaṇapāṭha (noun groups).
  • He made a special metalanguage with short codes, so thousands of forms fit into a few rules.
  • His method is algorithmic: take a root, add a suffix, apply rules step by step, get the final word. It is very similar to BNF / context-free grammar in computer science, which is why it is studied for NLP and AI.
  • It made Sanskrit precise and unambiguous, and new words can be created whenever needed.
  • Later grammarians (Kātyāyana, Patañjali) built on it, and it shaped linguistics in India and abroad.

  1. Pāṇini: wrote the Aṣṭādhyāyī (the rules)
  2. Kātyāyana (~4th c. BCE): wrote Vārttikas that clarify and add to Pāṇini's rules
  3. Patañjali (~2nd c. BCE): wrote the Mahābhāṣya, the "great commentary"

  2. Aṣṭādhyāyī: the core rules for forming words and sentences.
  3. Dhātupāṭha: a dictionary of verb roots, classified by meaning and use.
  4. Gaṇapāṭha: lists of groups of noun stems that follow the same rule.

What are the Maheśvara Sūtras?

They are 14 short sūtras at the start of Pāṇini's grammar that list all the sounds of Sanskrit in a special order. Tradition says they came from the beats of Lord Śiva's (Naṭarāja's) ḍamaru. They let Pāṇini refer to a whole group of sounds with one short code, so they are the base of Vyākaraṇa.

2.2 Aṣṭādhyāyī's Vyākaraṇa (How the Grammar Works) ⭐⭐⭐

📘 Notes in simple words

Structure: Aṣṭa (8) + adhyāya (chapter). 8 chapters × 4 pādas (quarters) = 32 sections, with about 3983 sūtras.

Big idea: language is a collection of words, and Sanskrit words are made by rules, not memorised.

Dhātu (verb root) + Pratyaya (suffix) → Kriyā-pada (verb form)
Prātipadika (noun stem) + Pratyaya (suffix) → Nāma-pada (noun form)

A verb can be turned into a noun with a suffix (do → doer, kṛ → kartā), and a noun can be turned into a verb (putra → putrīyati, "wants a son"). Examples: karoti, kartā, putrīyati, rāmaḥ, ramate.

  • Step-by-step and recursive: at each step, a rule applies if its condition matches. This continues until no rule applies, and you get the final correct word.
  • Modular: base + suffix combinations make patterns across the whole vocabulary.
  • Indicators: Pāṇini attaches marker letters to roots. Example: the root is listed as "ḍukṛñ"; remove "ḍu" and "ñ" to get kṛ (to do). Karma also comes from kṛ.
  • Counting forms: nouns: 7 cases × 3 numbers (singular, dual, plural) = 21 suffixes. Verbs: 3 persons × 3 numbers = 9 per tense.
  • Learn one root's pattern and you can use it for about 2200 verb roots.
  • Dynamic vocabulary: new valid words can always be made by following the rules, which keeps Sanskrit adaptable over centuries.
  • Modern significance: it works like a formal generative grammar: rule-driven, logical and algorithmic. That makes it suitable for computers, computational linguistics and AI. It is the world's oldest and most complete descriptive model of a language.

  • Aṣṭa = eight, adhyāya = chapter, so it is the "book of 8 chapters".
  • Each chapter has 4 pādas (quarters), so 8 × 4 = 32 sections.
  • In total there are about 3,983 sūtras (rules), roughly 4,000.
  • The rules are very short and work like formulas. A word from one rule carries over into the next rules (anuvṛtti), which keeps it compact.

Likely · 3M⭐⭐
Why is Sanskrit called a rule-based language? What is the modern significance of the Aṣṭādhyāyī?

Rule-based:

  • Every word is formed by rules: a root plus suffixes.
  • If you apply the right rules, you always get the correct word. There is no ambiguity.
  • Derivation is step-by-step and recursive: apply a rule when its condition matches, and stop when no rule applies.

Modern significance:

  • It is like a formal generative grammar (CFG / BNF).
  • Logical and algorithmic, so it is suitable for computer processing, NLP and AI.
  • Dynamic vocabulary: new words can be made as needed, which matters for word creation and dictionaries.
  • It is the oldest and most complete descriptive model of any language.

Likely · 3–5M⭐⭐⭐
Explain the place (sthāna) and effort (prayatna) of pronunciation in Sanskrit phonetics.

Sanskrit phonetics classifies every sound by where it is made (sthāna) and how it is made (prayatna).

1. Sthāna (6 places):

  • Throat (kaṇṭha): a, k-group, h
  • Palate (tālu): i, c-group, y, ś
  • Upper palate (mūrdhā): ṛ, ṭ-group, r, ṣ
  • Teeth (danta): ḷ, t-group, l, s
  • Lips (oṣṭha): u, p-group
  • Nose (nāsikā): ṅ, ñ, ṇ, n, m

2. Prayatna (effort):

  • Vowels: by length (short, long, elongated), by nasality (nasal, non-nasal), and by pitch (udātta high, anudātta low, svarita mixed).
  • Consonants: alpa-prāṇa (less breath: k, g) and mahā-prāṇa (more breath: kh, gh).

Why it matters: in Vedic chanting, a change of sound changes the meaning and the result, so pronunciation had to be exact.

Likely · 2–3M⭐⭐
Why was phonetics (Śikṣā) so important in the Vedic tradition?
  • The Vedas were passed on orally, so exact pronunciation was the only way to protect them.
  • A small change in sound, accent or length can change the meaning and effect of a mantra.
  • Preserving the Vedas was seen as a sacred duty.
  • The Śikṣāvallī lists 6 aspects of correct recitation: Varṇa (letter), Svara (tone), Mātrā (length), Balam (force), Sāma (evenness), Santāna (continuity).

Dimensions of language processing (2 × 2):

  • Sound: listening and speaking, handled by phonetics.
  • Script: reading and writing, handled by syntax and grammar.

Linguistics covers both sound and script.
